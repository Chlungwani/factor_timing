Chapter 4: Results

4.1 Overview of the evaluation framework

This chapter presents the final out-of-sample results for the prediction
of relative leadership among the momentum, value and quality factors in
the South African equity market. Three model classes are evaluated:
regularised logistic regression, a generalised additive model (GAM), and
extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). These models provide a progression
from a linear specification, through additive nonlinear effects, to a
flexible tree-based model capable of representing nonlinearities and
interactions.

Hyperparameters are selected using combinatorial purged cross-validation
(CPCV) with five chronologically ordered groups and two groups held out
in each combination. This produces ten training-validation combinations
and four back-test paths. A 12-week purge before, and a 12-week embargo
after, each held-out CPCV group addresses the overlapping information
horizon introduced by the trend-scanning labels. Final performance is
evaluated using a separate expanding walk-forward procedure with a
12-week pre-test purge. Each model is initially trained on 751 eligible
observations, with the training sample expanding to 906 observations by
the end of the evaluation period. Hyperparameters are reconsidered every
52 predictions.

The final evaluation block contains 156 weekly observations from 31
January 2022 to 20 January 2025. All models are evaluated on the same
dates and use a fixed probability threshold of 0.5. The positive class
indicates leadership by the first-named factor. For example, a positive
momentum-versus-value observation indicates that the trend-scanning
label favours momentum over value.

Performance is assessed using accuracy, ROC-AUC and log-loss. Accuracy
reports the proportion of correct classifications, ROC-AUC measures
ranking ability independently of the 0.5 threshold, and log-loss
evaluates the quality of the predicted probabilities. Classification
errors are examined through consolidated confusion-matrix counts because
several specifications produce asymmetric class predictions. No separate
benchmark comparison is undertaken.

4.3.1 Momentum versus value

Momentum versus value provides the strongest classification result in
the study. XGBoost achieves the highest accuracy (61.5%) and ROC-AUC
(0.589) for this pair. The GAM ranks second, with accuracy of 59.0% and
ROC-AUC of 0.582.

The point estimates therefore indicate that nonlinear structure
contributes to this factor pair. The size of the effect remains moderate
because the best AUC is below 0.60. The results support a relative
advantage for XGBoost within this pair, but do not indicate strong
separation between momentum- and value-leadership regimes.

Logistic regression performs poorly at the fixed threshold, with
accuracy of 48.1% and ROC-AUC of 0.551. Its comparatively low log-loss
of 0.688 indicates more conservative probabilities, but the confusion
matrix shows a strong tendency to predict momentum leadership.

[Final walk-forward probabilities for momentum versus value]

4.3.2 Momentum versus quality

Predictive performance is weaker for momentum versus quality. XGBoost
again produces the strongest overall classification results, with
accuracy of 55.1% and ROC-AUC of 0.578. These values indicate only
modest separation between the two classes.

Logistic regression records the lowest log-loss, at 0.687, but its
accuracy is only 51.3%. The confusion matrix shows that it predicts the
positive class frequently, so its relatively strong detection of
momentum-leadership observations does not represent consistent
discrimination between both regimes.

The GAM displays the opposite error pattern. Its ROC-AUC of 0.557
suggests limited ranking information, but it predicts the positive class
infrequently. Its accuracy of 48.1% remains consistent with chance-like
classification.

[Final walk-forward probabilities for momentum versus quality]

4.3.3 Value versus quality

Value versus quality reverses the ordering observed in the momentum
pairs. Logistic regression is the strongest model, with accuracy of
56.4%, ROC-AUC of 0.585 and log-loss of 0.684. Its predicted class
distribution is also closest to the realised distribution for this pair.

XGBoost produces a ROC-AUC of 0.565, but this ranking result does not
translate into useful classification at the fixed threshold. It predicts
value leadership for 93.6% of the evaluation observations, despite a
realised positive rate of 47.4%. Its accuracy is therefore 48.7%, below
both logistic regression and the GAM. The result demonstrates why the
ROC-AUC should be interpreted alongside the confusion matrix.

The GAM is close to chance across the reported measures, with accuracy
of 51.9% and ROC-AUC of 0.521. The evidence for predictable
value-versus-quality leadership is therefore concentrated in the linear
specification rather than in the more flexible models.

Table 4.3 averages the three reported metrics across the factor pairs.
XGBoost records the highest mean accuracy and ROC-AUC. The average
advantage is nevertheless modest, and it is not observed uniformly
across the individual factor pairs. Logistic regression performs best

the GAM does not lead any pair on the principal performance measures.

These results provide qualified evidence in favour of XGBoost: it has
the strongest average accuracy and ranking performance and leads both

Logistic regression leads value versus quality and produces
substantially better probability quality than the GAM and, on average,
XGBoost. The model hierarchy is therefore dependent on the target pair
and the aspect of performance being evaluated.

XGBoost provides the most balanced class treatment for the two momentum
pairs. For momentum versus value, it predicts the positive class in
59.6% of observations compared with an actual rate of 54.5%. For
momentum versus quality, its positive prediction share is 46.8% compared
with an actual rate of 53.2%. Logistic regression is most balanced for
value versus quality, where its positive prediction rate is 52.6%
compared with the actual rate of 47.4%.

The extreme positive-class tendencies of logistic regression for
momentum versus value and XGBoost for value versus quality produce high
positive-class detection rates but poor specificity. These cases show
why aggregate performance measures should be interpreted alongside the
full error counts.

The probability results introduce a further trade-off. An uninformative
forecast of 0.5 for every observation has a log-loss of approximately
0.693. Logistic regression records log-loss between 0.684 and 0.688 for
all three pairs, representing only a small improvement but avoiding
severe overconfidence. The GAM produces log-loss between 0.932 and
1.609, indicating that some incorrect forecasts receive excessive
probability. XGBoost has stronger average ranking and thresholded
performance, but its log-loss exceeds 0.693 for both momentum pairs.
Thus, better discrimination does not necessarily imply better
probability quality.

4.5 Global SHAP analysis

Global feature importance is measured using the mean absolute SHAP value
from the saved model explanations. The values indicate which inputs
exerted the greatest influence on each fitted model. They do not
establish causal relationships or demonstrate that the variables contain
stable out-of-sample information. This distinction is important because
all final ROC-AUC values remain below 0.60. Rankings are compared more
cautiously than raw SHAP magnitudes because the scale and distribution
of attributions differ across model classes.

4.5.4 Cross-pair feature synthesis

Three broad patterns emerge from the SHAP rankings. First, pair-specific
relative-return and relative-volatility measures recur frequently,
indicating that the models rely on the recent state and dispersion of
the factor relationships. Second, market-state variables - including
currency volatility, yield-curve shape, volatility-term-structure and
commodity-regime indicators - contribute across several specifications.
Third, the importance rankings vary by model class and factor pair. The
results do not reveal a single universal economic variable that drives
factor leadership across all nine fitted models.

Because the predictive performance is modest, feature attribution
provides exploratory evidence about model behaviour rather than
confirmation of robust forecasting mechanisms. Stability analysis across
retuning windows would be required before claiming that the rankings
persist through time.

4.6 Validation, reproducibility and limits of inference

The final CPCV design strengthens the internal consistency of the
analysis by dividing each training sample into five chronologically
ordered groups and holding out two groups per combination. The resulting
ten training-validation combinations form four back-test paths. A
12-week purge before and a 12-week embargo after each CPCV test group
limit overlap with the trend-scanning information horizon. The separate
expanding walk-forward evaluation maintains chronological ordering,
applies a 12-week pre-test purge and retunes the models every 52
predictions. The final comparison is therefore based on common target
definitions, predictors, dates and validation settings.

Several limitations remain. First, the evaluation block contains only
156 weekly observations, while the trend-scanning labels display
temporal persistence. Small differences in point estimates should
therefore not be interpreted as statistically established without
dependence-aware confidence intervals or paired tests. Second, the same
evaluation period has been consulted during model development, meaning
that it should not be described as a completely untouched final holdout.
Third, the current saved artifacts contain the initial parameter
selection, but do not preserve every parameter choice made during the
two subsequent 52-week retuning events. Those retuning dates, training
windows, CPCV scores and selected parameters should be exported before
final submission. Fourth, the analysis evaluates statistical
classification and does not establish portfolio profitability after
turnover, transaction costs and implementation constraints.

4.7 Chapter conclusion

The final results provide modest and factor-pair-dependent evidence of
predictability. XGBoost produces the strongest results for momentum
versus value and momentum versus quality, while logistic regression
performs best for value versus quality. XGBoost also records the highest
mean accuracy and ROC-AUC across the three pairs. Its average advantage
does not amount to uniform dominance: logistic regression has the
strongest probability quality and leads the value-versus-quality pair,
while several models produce strongly asymmetric classifications at the
0.5 threshold.

The findings therefore qualify the proposition that increasing model
flexibility necessarily improves out-of-sample prediction. Greater
flexibility is beneficial for the two momentum pairs, particularly
momentum versus value, but the linear model is more effective for value
versus quality. Even the best AUC is below 0.60. The evidence supports
limited predictive structure rather than a strongly separable or
deployment-ready factor-timing signal.

The global SHAP analysis indicates that the fitted models use a
combination of relative-factor dynamics, volatility, currency
conditions, valuation, yield-curve information, commodity regimes and
sentiment. These patterns differ across model classes and factor pairs
and should remain exploratory given the modest predictive performance.
